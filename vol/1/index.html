    How to benchmark RSI

    How much of realistic LLM training do today's benchmark environments cover?

    Recursive self-improvement (RSI) could have major consequences for AI development, yet its definition, its possible forms and its measurement remain open questions. Recent reports from Anthropic, OpenAI and Z.ai describe AI systems taking on parts of model research and infrastructure engineering, which makes these questions increasingly practical. Whatever definition prevails, the ability to train and improve AI models end to end is a core capability, and it is necessary, though not sufficient, for RSI. One way to evaluate progress toward RSI is to ask an AI system to do exactly that: develop and train a model and deliver it ready for real-world use at production scale. However, such an evaluation would be expensive in both compute and time at the moment.

    Meanwhile, coding benchmarks such as SWE-Bench Pro, Terminal-Bench, DeepSWE, ProgramBench and SWE-Serve assess progress in coding agents, and they are built from task environments that also support agent training. A task environment is a containerized snapshot of a server, a developer machine or a simulated world, prepared with an initial state, the required artifacts and, usually, a verifiable goal, so that an agent can be rolled out in it repeatedly against a realistic problem without escaping its sandbox. These benchmarks cover important software-engineering and general machine-learning problems; how well their task environments represent the work of developing production LLMs is a separate question, and one we have not seen checked. Such task environments could, in principle, test the capabilities an end-to-end training run requires, one at a time.

    In this post, we investigate how much production-level infrastructure knowledge for LLM training is covered by existing public task environments.

    01

    What is needed for end-to-end LLM training

    We organize the audit along two dimensions: five stages of the LLM development and deployment life cycle, and seven system layers. The classification synthesizes documented model-development pipelines and the architectures of production frameworks. The categories overlap: data and evaluation recur throughout development, agent post-training is the multi-turn, sandboxed and largely off-policy part of post-training, and infrastructure responsibilities span several components. We assign coverage by what each task environment's verifier exercises, and grade each cell by how deeply: the real LLM workload executed, bounded unit or artifact tests, or related and simulated evidence.

    02

    Where public benchmark environments land

    Each row below is a benchmark. The marks show which of the 35 stage × layer cells its task environments reach, and how deeply; the bar sums them into one coverage score; the bottom row counts task environments per stage.

    We read every public task environment in eleven benchmarks and audited each one that touches the LLM life cycle (task census). We find that:

    1. Across the general-purpose coding benchmarks, only a small fraction of task environments test an agent's ability to work on LLM training itself: 9 of 66 in Terminal-Bench 4.0, 9 of 89 in Terminal-Bench 2.1 and 3 of 113 in DeepSWE. Across all eleven benchmarks, 150 of 590 public task environments (25%) touch any part of the LLM life cycle, and 53 of those are the serving tasks of SWE-Serve.
    2. Across the whole LLM training life cycle, only a small part of the infrastructure knowledge is exercised by any task environment: the eleven benchmarks together run a real LLM workload in 12 of the 35 stage × layer cells, no single benchmark does so in more than 10, and the data and agent post-training stages and the cluster layer have no task environment that runs the real system at all.
    3. The specialized benchmarks provide substantial training and inference coverage, but their task environments largely test individual components or bounded experiments, leaving gaps in coordinated, end-to-end workflows. SWE-Serve is the sharpest case: 21 of its 53 verifiers run a real SGLang engine, every one on a single GPU.
    03

    What full-cycle LLM training looks like, from open sources

    If benchmarks cover so little, is that because the full cycle is hidden inside frontier labs? Maybe not. We cannot see how proprietary models are trained, but open checkpoints, technical reports, released training code and, lately, live-streamed training runs have made the whole cycle visible on the open side. Several open models now compete with frontier proprietary ones, and they were trained on open-source frameworks: GLM-5 on slime and Nemotron 3 on NeMo-RL, and Xiaomi even streamed MiMo-V2.6's RL run to a public dashboard. Reviewing these materials, the full cycle falls into the same five stages we audit against, from data through pretraining and post-training to agent post-training and deployment.

    04

    Future challenges

    Section 02 showed that today's task environments cover little of end-to-end LLM training, and that the real system is never executed for data, agent post-training or cluster work. That is not carelessness on the benchmark builders' part. Training a production model takes large data, many GPUs across several nodes and long runs whose failures are distributed and slow to surface, and a task environment that must be built, run and graded in minutes on one machine cannot hold most of that. The ceiling is the container, not the task author. Two directions look hard but rewarding.

    Tasks shaped like the work. The Terminal-Bench series shows that a community can produce hundreds of executable, verifiable task environments in a shared format: each is an instruction, a container and a test script, and Harbor turned that format into a pipeline, with adapters for other benchmarks, a hub for hosting datasets and jobs, and the same trials reused as training rollouts. That workflow is the right one for LLM-training task environments too, and the interest is there: MLS-Bench's 28 training and serving task environments, RSI-Exam's kernel and post-training environments, and PostTrainBench's ten-H100-hour post-training task all appeared within a year. What is still missing are task environments shaped like the work itself, where the agent must move a real training or serving system through a realistic problem, such as recovering a run after a failed node, keeping an asynchronous RL loop within a staleness budget, or publishing weights to a fleet of rollout engines, rather than editing one function behind a fixed harness.

    Environments sized like the work. The environments in the figure are small by construction: of its 127 task environments, 43 declare no GPU at all, 55 declare one, 25 declare two to four, three declare five to eight, one (the EdgeBench family) does not document hardware per task, and none spans more than one node. Terminal-Bench 2.1 and DeepSWE declare zero GPUs for every task environment, Terminal-Bench 4.0 gives three of its 66 a single H100, SWE-Serve caps every one of its 53 at one H100 by design, and the largest environment anywhere in the set is RE-Bench's eight H100s, while the smallest documented production pretraining run in our sources uses 384. A task environment cannot ask for a multi-GPU node, let alone several, if the harness cannot provision, meter and tear one down. That is changing. Harbor's task format already declares CPUs, memory, storage and GPU counts and supports multi-container Compose environments and separate verifier containers; sandbox providers such as Modal and Daytona now run Harbor trials remotely, and SkyRL uses them for RL rollouts at hundreds of concurrent sandboxes. The next step is environments that hold a node or a small cluster for hours, expose real interconnects and schedulers, and grade what happens to a training run, not only what a unit test sees.

    None of this needs a new idea, only more of the work the community is already doing: task environments written against real training and serving stacks, and harnesses that can hold a node for hours.
